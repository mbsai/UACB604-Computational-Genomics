Each added nucleotide is a terminator with probability \(q\). A fragment stops at length \(L\) if \(L-1\) normal bases are followed by one terminator:

$$P(L) = (1-q)^{L-1}\,q$$

This is the geometric distribution, the same maths as stop-codon runs in Lecture 1.1. Mean length \(= 1/q\).

6 min. Move the slider: too much ddNTP (q large) → short fragments only; too little → hardly anything stops early, weak signal everywhere. Best q for position L is 1/L (calculus: notebook exercise 3).

Phred quality scores

The base-calling program reports an error probability \(p\) for every base, on a log scale:

$$Q = -10\log_{10} p \qquad p = 10^{-Q/10}$$

The read-length limit maths

Why Sanger reads stop at ~1,000 bases

1 · Signal fades

Fragments of length \(L\) make up a fraction \((1-q)^{L-1}q\) of all fragments. With \(q = 1/300\):

  • length 100: 0.24% of fragments
  • length 1,000: 0.012%, about 20× weaker

2 · Peaks blur

Electrophoresis must separate fragments of length \(L\) and \(L+1\). The relative difference is

$$\frac{1}{L}: \quad 1\% \text{ at } L = 100,\quad 0.1\% \text{ at } L = 1000$$

Peaks broaden as fragments travel further, and eventually neighbours merge.

Result: high-quality reads of about 700–1,000 bases, with the best quality in the middle. (The first ~30 bases are messy too, near the primer.)

Still in use biology

Where Sanger sequencing still wins

Strengths

  • Very accurate (Q40+ in the middle of a good read)
  • Simple: one PCR product, one read, results in hours
  • The “gold standard” to confirm a variant found by other methods
  • Checking plasmid clones and CRISPR edits

Limits

  • One sequence per reaction: a 96-capillary machine yields ~100 kb per run
  • A mixture of molecules gives overlapping traces (but this also reveals heterozygotes: two peaks at one position)
  • Far too slow and costly for whole genomes today: the Human Genome Project took 13 years

Today in one slide

  • ddNTPs stop chains at random → fragment lengths are geometric, mean \(1/q\).
