Prokaryotes biology maths

Bacteria: compact, co-oriented, clustered

  • Usually one circular chromosome, plus small circular plasmids.
  • No introns. ~86% of E. coli codes for protein.
  • Operons: neighbouring genes on the same strand, read into one mRNA (e.g. lacZYA).
  • Genes prefer the leading strand of replication.

Testable predictions

If gene orientation were random:

  • P(two neighbours on the same strand) = 0.5
  • P(gene on leading strand) = 0.5

In E. coli (you'll measure this in the notebook):

70.8%neighbours on same strand
54.8%genes on leading strand

With 4,318 genes, random orientation would give 50% ± 1.5%. Both are far outside that.

The most common gap between same-strand neighbours is −4 bp: the stop codon TGA of one gene overlaps the start ATG of the next, as in …ATGA… read two ways. That is an operon fingerprint you can detect with a computer.

5 min. Write ATGA on the board: frame 1 reads …TGA (stop), the next gene starts at ATG one base earlier → overlap of 4.
Eukaryotes interactive maths

Exons, introns and arithmetic mod 3

A gene with 6 exons. Exons 2–5 can be skipped by alternative splicing. Untick to skip:

mRNA coding length
reading frame

Codons are read in threes. Skipping an exon of length \(\ell\) keeps the protein in frame only if $$\ell \equiv 0 \pmod 3$$ Otherwise every codon after it is garbled, usually hitting a stop soon.

Real record: fruit-fly Dscam has 4 clusters of alternative exons (12 × 48 × 33 × 2) = 38,016 possible proteins from one gene.

7 min. Exon lengths: 132 | 120, 98, 150, 77 | 209 (total 786 = 262 codons). Exons 2–5 optional. Residues mod 3: 0, 2, 0, 2. Frame-keeping subsets: {}, {2}, {4}, {2,4} → 4 of 16. Skipping both 98 and 77 removes 175 bp ≡ 1 (mod 3), so it still shifts.

Intron lengths: why the mean misleads

Human intron lengths are strongly right-skewed: most are a few kb, a few are over 100 kb. A log-normal model: \(\log L \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\).

spread σ =
median
mean

$$\text{median} = e^{\mu},\qquad \text{mean} = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$$

Report medians for lengths, counts and expression. One 1 Mb intron can drag the mean far from a typical intron.

4 min. Median fixed at 1.5 kb (μ = ln 1500). At σ = 1.2, mean ≈ 1.5 kb × e^0.72 ≈ 3.1 kb. Ask: what happens to the mean when σ doubles?
What fills a big genome? biology

Nearly half of our genome is old transposons

  • LINEs (e.g. L1): long, can copy themselves.
  • SINEs (e.g. Alu): ~300 bp, about 1 million copies, ~10% of the genome on their own.
  • LTR elements: remnants of retroviruses.

Fractions from the initial human genome paper (Lander et al., Nature 2001).

Link to Lecture 1.1: we computed that 16-mers should be unique in a random 3 Gb genome. A million near-identical Alu copies break that assumption. This is why assembling human genomes from short reads is hard (Units 5–7).

Genomes inside genomes biology

Organelle genomes look bacterial

Human mitochondrial DNA

16,569 bp
  • Circular, no introns, genes on both strands, almost no gaps
  • 37 genes: 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs
  • Hundreds to thousands of copies per cell; inherited from the mother

Chloroplast DNA

~120–160 kb
  • Circular, with operon-like gene clusters
Both descend from engulfed bacteria (endosymbiosis). The evidence is phylogenetic: their genes cluster with bacterial genes on evolutionary trees (Unit 9).
2 min. Maths aside: mtDNA's high copy number means it is over-represented in sequencing data — a coverage spike they will see in Unit 5.

Wrap-up

Today in one slide

  • Bacteria: ~1 gene per kb, ~86% coding, operons, leading-strand bias. Each is a testable statistical signal.
  • Human: ~6.5 genes per Mb, ~1.2% coding; genes are islands of exons in introns and repeats.
  • Alternative splicing is combinatorics plus arithmetic mod 3.
  • Length data are skewed: prefer medians and log scales.
  • Repeats break "random string" assumptions, which matters for assembly.
