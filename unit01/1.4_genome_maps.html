Two genes, 50 Mb apart on one chromosome

You inherit one copy of this chromosome from your mother. She got her two copies from her parents, and during meiosis they swapped segments (crossing over).

Two maps of the same chromosome

  • Physical map: distance in base pairs (bp, Mb). Fixed by the DNA sequence.
  • Genetic (linkage) map: distance in centiMorgans (cM). 1 cM ≈ 1% chance of a crossover between two points per meiosis.

Like a city map with distances in km vs in minutes of travel: related, but not proportional.

Recombination fraction: $$r = \frac{\#\text{recombinants}}{\#\text{gametes}}$$

Sturtevant (1913, as an undergraduate) had the key idea: if \(r\) grows with distance, then \(r\) values can place genes in order along a line. That was the first genetic map, decades before anyone knew what DNA was.
4 min. r = 170/1000 = 0.17, i.e. ~17 cM for small distances.

Derivation maths

Haldane's map function in four lines

Assume: crossovers form a Poisson process with rate 1 per Morgan (1 M = 100 cM). Between two markers at map distance \(d\) (in M), the number of crossovers \(K\) is Poisson:

$$P(K = k) = e^{-d}\frac{d^k}{k!}$$

Recombinant ⇔ \(K\) odd: $$r = \sum_{k\ \text{odd}} e^{-d}\frac{d^k}{k!} = e^{-d}\sinh d$$

$$\boxed{\,r = \tfrac12\left(1 - e^{-2d}\right)\,} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; d = -\tfrac12\ln(1-2r)$$

Check the limits

  • Small \(d\): \(e^{-2d}\approx 1-2d\), so \(r \approx d\). Close genes: cM ≈ % recombinants.
  • Large \(d\): \(e^{-2d}\to 0\), so \(r \to \tfrac12\). Far genes look unlinked.
Warm-up answer: 50 Mb ≈ 50 cM = 0.5 M in humans (about 1 cM per Mb). \(r = \tfrac12(1-e^{-1}) = 0.316\), so the two genes stay together in ~68% of children.
5 min. Write on board: sinh d = (e^d − e^−d)/2 = Σ odd d^k/k!. Multiply by e^−d: (1 − e^−2d)/2.
Why we convert interactive maths

Distances add; recombination fractions don't

Genes A–B–C in that order with \(r_{AB} = r_{BC} = 0.10\).

Map distances do add: \(d_{AC} = d_{AB} + d_{BC}\). That is why maps are drawn in cM, not in \(r\).

Real crossovers repel each other (interference). Kosambi's map function allows for it: $$d_K = \tfrac14\ln\frac{1+2r}{1-2r}$$

Genetic vs physical biology

cM per Mb is not constant

Schematic “Marey map” of one chromosome: genetic position vs physical position. Slope = local recombination rate.

  • Human average: roughly 1 cM per Mb.
  • Hotspots: most crossovers fall in short stretches of 1–2 kb (in humans and mice, positioned by the PRDM9 protein).
  • Near centromeres: very little recombination (flat).
  • Sex difference: the female human map is roughly 1.6× longer than the male map.
So converting cM to Mb with one number is only a first approximation, but a useful one.

Physical maps, then and now biology

From linkage to sequence

Physical mapping methods

  • Restriction maps: positions of enzyme cut sites
  • STS maps: short unique sequences ordered along clones (STS = sequence-tagged site; uses the k-mer uniqueness idea from 1.1)
  • FISH: fluorescent probes seen on chromosomes
  • Optical maps / Hi-C: modern long-range maps used to order assembly pieces (Units 6–7)
  • The complete sequence is the ultimate physical map

Why maps mattered

  • Huntington's disease was mapped by linkage to chromosome 4 in 1983, ten years before the gene was found.
  • The cystic fibrosis gene (CFTR) was found in 1989 by mapping first, then walking along chromosome 7.
  • Today: genetic maps guide genome assembly and association studies.
4 min. This is “positional cloning”: map → narrow region → sequence candidates.

Today in one slide

  • Recombination fraction \(r\) = recombinant gametes ÷ all gametes; never above 0.5.
  • Poisson crossovers ⇒ Haldane: \(r = \tfrac12(1-e^{-2d})\). Kosambi adds interference.
  • Map distances (cM) add; \(r\) values don't.
  • Three-point crosses: the rarest classes reveal the middle gene.
  • Genetic and physical maps disagree locally (hotspots, centromeres, sex).
